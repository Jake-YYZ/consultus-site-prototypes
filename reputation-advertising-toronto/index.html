What we run

The whole reputation system.

Asking well and at the right moment, answering everything, your Google profile, the third-party sites that matter in your category, your brand search page, and monitoring.

Review generation

The whole programme rests on asking well, at the right moment, every time.

We work out when your customer is most satisfied, which is rarely at the point of payment, and build the ask into that moment by text, email, or a counter card with a QR code. Then we remove the friction: one tap, no login, straight to the platform that matters most for your category.

Volume matters less than steadiness. A handful of reviews every week beats forty in one month and silence after.

Review response and recovery

Every review gets an answer, including the good ones.

Negative reviews get a reply that acknowledges the specific problem, says what changed, and moves the conversation offline. Done properly this often earns an edit or an upgrade, and the people it really persuades are the hundreds reading later.

A defensive or copy-pasted reply does more damage than the original complaint.

Google Business Profile

For local searches this is the page that decides, not your website.

Categories, services, hours, attributes, and photos get set up properly and kept current. We use Q&A to answer the objections that come up on sales calls, and post regularly, because a stale profile quietly slides down the map pack.

Profiles also get watched for hijacked edits and duplicate listings, which are more common than most businesses realise.

Third-party and industry profiles

Google is where most people look first. It is rarely where they stop.

We identify the two or three other places your buyers actually check, whether that is Facebook, Trustpilot, Yelp, or an industry directory like RateMDs or Houzz, and keep those consistent. Contradictory hours and phone numbers across profiles cost you both trust and local ranking.

We do not chase every directory that exists. Most add nothing.

Brand search control

Search your own company name. Whatever sits on that first page is your reputation.

Complaint-site pages and old news can outrank your own properties for your brand. The durable fix is publishing and strengthening assets you own, so your site, profiles, case studies, and press occupy that page instead.

This takes months, not weeks. Start before you need it.

Monitoring and alerts

You should hear about a one star review from us, not from a customer.

New reviews and brand mentions get monitored across the platforms that matter, with alerts routed to whoever can actually act. We track the themes as well as the ratings, because five people mentioning the same delay is an operations problem, not a marketing one.

That feedback loop is often the most valuable part of the engagement.

How we work

Earned slowly, then defended.

Reputation work is a habit, not a campaign. The first month is mostly diagnosis, and some of what we find will be operational rather than marketing.

01

Audit

We pull your ratings, review velocity, and response rate across every platform your buyers check, then search your brand name and record what actually appears. You also get the themes hiding in your existing reviews, which is usually the most uncomfortable and most useful part.

02

Fix the source

If the reviews are telling you something true about wait times, a product, or one location, no amount of asking will outrun it. We flag what has to change operationally before the programme starts, because campaigns can't outvote reality.

03

Build the ask

The request gets built into the moment of highest satisfaction and made as frictionless as possible, with response templates and an escalation path so nothing sits unanswered for days.

04

Hold the line

Reputation decays. Reviews age, competitors keep collecting, and profiles drift out of date. We keep the cadence steady and report on ratings, velocity, response times, and what your brand search page looks like.

Expect the audit in the first two weeks and a working ask in month one. Ratings move slowly at first, because an average is hard to shift until new reviews outnumber the old ones. Businesses starting from a low volume usually see the average move within two to three months. If you're digging out of a serious problem, plan in quarters.

The scoreboard

Which numbers actually matter.

The star average is the number everyone asks about and the one we watch least closely.

Star average

The number buyers see, and the one worth the least attention day to day.

It is a lagging average, so it moves slowly and tells you nothing about why. Useful as a target, useless as a weekly metric.

Review velocity

How many new reviews you earn per month, per location.

This is the number we actually manage. Recency is weighted by both buyers and Google, and steady flow beats occasional bursts every time.

Response rate and time

The share of reviews answered, and how quickly.

Aim for everything answered, negatives within a day. It is the clearest public signal that somebody is paying attention.

Brand search page

What appears when someone searches your company name.

We track which results you own versus which belong to third parties and complaint sites. Progress here is slow and it is the most durable protection you can build.

Review themes

The recurring subjects inside the text, not the ratings.

Five mentions of the same delay is an operations finding. This is the part of the reporting that tends to change how the business runs.

Conversion lift

What the ratings do to enquiries and calls.

Star ratings show in map results and ads, so they change click-through and call volume. If reputation work is not moving enquiries, it is decoration.

Diagnostics

Gating and buying always backfire.

Shortcuts that look efficient and cost more than they save.

Review gating

Screening customers and only sending the happy ones to Google breaks Google's own policy and risks the whole review set. It is also unnecessary. A well-timed ask to everyone usually produces a better average than gating did.

Buying reviews

Detection is far better than it was, penalties hit your profile, never the vendor, and one exposed batch costs more trust than years of honest collecting earns.

Only asking after a complaint

Businesses tend to remember reviews when something goes wrong, which means the profile fills up with recovery stories and nothing else. Ask when things go right, which is most of the time.

The one-off blitz

Forty reviews in a fortnight then nothing looks artificial to buyers and to the platforms. Recency is weighted, so the burst decays and you're back where you started.

Arguing in public

A defensive reply confirms whatever the reviewer said to every future reader. Acknowledge, state what changed, take it offline.

Suppressing instead of fixing

Pushing a complaint page down the brand search results does nothing about the reason it was written. The theme resurfaces, usually on a platform you control even less.

Ratings decide whether people click and call at all. Improve the rating and every other channel gets cheaper, because the same traffic converts better. Nothing else on this page compounds as quietly.

Reviews are a ranking factor in the map pack, and the map pack is where local intent lands. Reviews and local rankings feed each other, which is why we run them together instead of as separate projects.

Paid traffic works while the organic position is still being earned. It also exposes the rating, since star extensions show in ads, so a weak profile makes every click more expensive.

In practice we run this alongside local SEO and Google Ads, because a strong profile makes both cheaper. If you have several locations, the SEO and reputation work have to be planned as one thing.

It connects

The themes in your reviews are the cheapest customer research you'll ever get, and they usually point at something fixable. We pass those findings to whoever owns the process, and where the problem is the website and not the service, that becomes conversion work instead.

FAQ

How fast can a rating move?

Can you remove negative reviews?
Usually not, and you should be wary of anyone who promises it. Platforms only take reviews down when they break their own rules, for example spam, a competitor, abusive content, or a customer who was never yours. We do report those and often win. Everything else gets answered, and a well-handled complaint sitting under a strong recent set of reviews does surprisingly little damage.
How much does reputation management cost?
It depends on the number of locations, how many platforms matter in your category, and whether we're maintaining a good position or digging out of a bad one. Recovery work costs more than upkeep. Ask us for a figure against your actual profile count, not a package price, and be suspicious of anything priced per review.
How long before our rating improves?
An average is stubborn, because old reviews stay in the maths. Businesses with low review volume often see movement in two to three months, since each new review carries real weight. If you have thousands of ratings, the average will barely move and the right target is velocity and recency instead. Serious recovery is a quarters-long project.
Is it against the rules to ask customers for reviews?
Asking is fine and Google encourages it. What isn't allowed is incentivising reviews, or gating, which means screening customers and only routing the happy ones to a public platform. Both put your profile at risk. We ask everyone, at a moment chosen so that most of the answers are good ones.
Do you write reviews for us?
No. Written reviews are detectable, the penalty lands on your profile, not on whoever wrote them, and the downside of being caught is far worse than a slow honest climb. If an agency offers this, it tells you what they will do to your brand when it is inconvenient.
Who replies to the reviews, you or us?
Either, and it depends on how technical the answers need to be. We usually draft and you approve for the first month, then most clients hand over routine replies entirely and keep escalation for anything involving a refund, a clinical detail, or a legal question. Whichever way, every review gets an answer.
What if the bad reviews are fair?
Then the reviews are doing their job and the fix isn't a marketing one. We will tell you what the recurring themes are and which are costing you the most enquiries. Running a review campaign over a real service problem just gets you more accurate bad reviews, faster.
Which platforms should we care about?
Google first for almost every local business, because it feeds both search and maps. After that it is category-specific: Facebook for consumer services, Trustpilot for ecommerce, and industry sites like RateMDs, Avvo, or Houzz where buyers check them. We pick two or three and keep them right instead of spreading thin across twenty directories.
Does this help with SEO?
Yes, particularly locally. Review count, ratings, recency, and responses all feed local ranking, and a stronger profile improves click-through from results you already hold. It is one of the reasons we run reputation and local SEO together instead of as separate projects.
What do you report on?
Ratings and review counts per location, review velocity, response rate and time, the themes coming up in the text, and what your brand search page looks like over time. The theme reporting is the part clients tend to find most useful, because it is the only one that tells you what to change.
