Creatine 101

What creatine does, and what it doesn't.

Creatine has more human research behind it than almost anything else on a supplement shelf, and it's still surrounded by more nonsense than almost anything else on a supplement shelf. Here's the plain version: how it works, what's genuinely proven, what's still being figured out, and how much to take.

What creatine is

Creatine is a compound your body already makes, roughly a gram a day, from three amino acids. You get more of it from food, mostly red meat and fish, at somewhere around one to two grams a day if you eat those regularly. About 95% of what you carry sits in skeletal muscle.

It isn't a stimulant, a hormone, or a steroid. It doesn't act on your nervous system and you won't feel it working. What it does is boost the size of a small, very fast energy reserve inside your muscle cells, and that reserve is the one you draw on during short, hard efforts.

The catch is that most people walk around with that reserve partly empty. Diet alone tends to leave muscle creatine stores somewhere below capacity, and lower still if you don't eat much meat.2 Supplementing fills the gap.

How it actually works

Your cells run on ATP. When you do anything hard, ATP gives up a phosphate group and becomes ADP, which is spent. You've got maybe a couple of seconds of ATP sitting around at any moment, so the useful question is how fast you can turn ADP back into ATP.

That's creatine's job. Stored in muscle as phosphocreatine, it hands its phosphate group straight to ADP and regenerates ATP almost instantly, faster than any other system in the body. More phosphocreatine in the tank means a faster recharge, which means less drop-off across a set and across a session.

Creatine doesn't give you energy. It gives you a faster way to get your energy back.

This is why the effect shows up where it does. It's most obvious in efforts lasting a few seconds to about half a minute, repeated with short rest: the fifth sprint, the third heavy set, the last two reps. It's least obvious in a steady one-hour run, where a different energy system is doing the work.

It's also why creatine is a slow build rather than a pre-workout. You're topping up a storage tank over weeks, not taking a dose that acts today.

What the research supports

Creatine monohydrate has been studied in humans since the early 1990s, and the International Society of Sports Nutrition's position stand remains the clearest summary of where the evidence sits.1 It's worth separating the settled findings from the promising ones, because a lot of marketing doesn't.

Well established

  • Strength and power output. The most reproducible result in the literature, particularly for repeated high-intensity efforts.1
  • Lean mass gains alongside resistance training. Partly from more total training work completed, partly from water drawn into the muscle cell early on.1
  • Recovery between efforts. Faster ATP resynthesis means less fade within a session.
  • Muscle retention in older adults. Studied specifically alongside resistance training in ageing populations, where the combination outperforms training alone.5

Promising but still developing

  • Cognition under stress. Your brain uses the same ATP system. Effects appear clearest when someone is sleep deprived or mentally fatigued, and much less so in rested, well-fed people.67
  • Hydration and heat tolerance. Creatine increases intracellular water, which is plausible for heat work but not yet settled.
  • Clinical and neurological applications. An active research area, well outside what a supplement brand should be making claims about.
The honest summary

If you train and you want to be stronger, creatine is one of the very few supplements with enough evidence to be worth the money. If you're hoping it will fix your sleep, your mood or your waistline, it won't, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something.

How much to take

Five grams a day of creatine monohydrate. That's the answer for the overwhelming majority of people, and it's the dose used across most of the research.1

Slightly larger people carry more muscle and therefore more storage capacity, so dosing is sometimes scaled by body weight at roughly 0.03 grams per kilogram per day. For a 90 kg person that lands at about 2.7 grams, which tells you how much margin five grams already has built in.

Standard 5 g per day, every day 3 to 4 weeks

Does timing matter?

Far less than consistency. Because you're filling a storage tank rather than taking something that acts acutely, what matters is the running total across weeks. Take it whenever you'll reliably remember. Taking it with food that contains carbohydrate or protein may modestly help uptake, which is a reasonable tiebreaker but not a rule.

Loading, or not

Loading means taking around 20 grams a day, split into four doses, for five to seven days before dropping to a maintenance dose. It works. It's just optional.

The classic comparison found that a low daily dose taken for 28 days produced muscle creatine levels equivalent to a six-day load followed by maintenance.3 Same destination, different speed. Loading gets you there in about a week instead of about a month.

Loading is a speed setting, not a requirement. And it's where nearly every stomach complaint comes from.

Load if you've got a competition or a training block starting in ten days. Otherwise skip it, take five grams, and let the first month do the work. Large single doses are the main driver of the bloating and GI upset people report, so spreading or skipping the load usually solves that complaint entirely.

Myths that won't die

Most of these trace back to a single misread study or to gym-floor telephone. The 2021 review addressing common creatine misconceptions is the best single place to see them handled properly.4

Myth 01

"Creatine damages your kidneys"

This comes from creatinine, a normal breakdown product of creatine that doctors use as a marker of kidney function. Supplementing raises the marker without indicating any damage, which is a measurement artefact rather than harm. Long-term studies in healthy adults have not found kidney damage at normal doses.14 Existing kidney disease is a genuine reason to speak to your doctor first.

Myth 02

"It makes you bloated and puffy"

Creatine draws water into muscle cells, which is different from holding water under the skin. Most people see fuller looking muscle rather than a soft look. Aggressive loading is what produces the temporary bloated feeling, and skipping the load usually removes it.

Myth 03

"You have to cycle off it"

There's no receptor to downregulate and your body doesn't stop making its own creatine because you supplement. Cycling just means your stores drain for a few weeks and you get less out of it during that window.4

Myth 04

"It causes hair loss"

This traces to one small 2009 study in rugby players that reported a rise in DHT, a hormone associated with male pattern baldness. It measured a hormone, not hair, and it hasn't been replicated. Calling it settled in either direction would be overstating what one study can show.4

Myth 05

"Creatine is only for men who lift"

The mechanism has nothing to do with sex, and the research in women and in older adults is growing steadily.5 Training is what unlocks most of the benefit, but "training" includes anyone doing resistance work at any age.

Myth 06

"Newer forms work better than monohydrate"

Hydrochloride, buffered, ethyl ester and liquid versions have all been marketed as upgrades. None has beaten monohydrate in head-to-head research.1 Monohydrate is the cheapest and the most studied, which is an unusual and welcome combination.

Who it's for

Anyone doing resistance training gets the clearest return, because the training is what converts the extra capacity into adaptation. Beyond that:

  • Athletes in repeated-sprint sports. Football, hockey, tennis, combat sports, anything with short bursts and short rest.
  • Adults over 40. The combination of creatine and resistance training is one of the better-supported strategies for holding on to muscle with age.5
  • Vegetarians and vegans. Baseline stores are typically lower without dietary meat, so the jump from supplementing tends to be larger.2
  • People who train while under-slept. The cognitive research is early, but this is where the signal is strongest.6

It's a poor fit if you're not training at all and expecting body composition change, or if you're looking for something you can feel. Neither is what creatine does.

Safety and side effects

Creatine monohydrate has one of the better safety records in supplementation, with studies running for years in healthy adults at normal doses.14 The side effects that do get reported are mostly digestive, and mostly the result of taking too much at once.

  • Stomach upset. Usually from large single doses. Split the dose or drop the load.
  • Early weight change. Typically one to two kilos of intracellular water in the first weeks. That's the mechanism working, not fat.
  • Nothing to feel. No jitters, no crash, no stimulant effect. If a creatine product makes you feel something, read the label again.

Talk to your doctor first if you have kidney disease or reduced kidney function, are pregnant or nursing, are under 18, or take prescription medication. That's a real caution, not legal boilerplate, and it's the one part of this page worth taking literally.
